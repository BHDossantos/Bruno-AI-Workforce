# OpsProof

**Product, Engineering, Architecture, Security, Data, AI and Design Execution Specification**

Version 1.0
Status: Internal team handoff and execution baseline
Scheduling model: Capability gates only; no calendar schedule is included
Working name: OpsProof is a working product name and requires legal, domain and trademark clearance before external launch

| Document control | Definition |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Define exactly what the team is building, why it matters, how it must behave, and the quality gates required before customer use. |
| Product category | Evidence-first production change risk, incident intelligence, human-approved remediation and operational resilience platform. |
| Initial customer | Regulated enterprises using Kubernetes, infrastructure as code, modern observability and formal incident/change processes. |
| Initial operating model | EU-hosted SaaS control plane with a customer-side read-only collector; private deployment is an enterprise option. |
| Primary decision rule | When scope, speed, AI novelty and safety conflict, choose evidence integrity, customer control and security. |

Founding product promise
OpsProof connects the customer's existing engineering and governance stack, calculates and explains production change risk, correlates incidents with likely causal changes, prepares controlled remediation, verifies recovery, and preserves a complete evidence trail. It does not replace observability, source control, CI/CD or ITSM systems.

## 1. Executive directive and non-negotiable decisions

### 1.1 The problem we are solving

Modern enterprises already own source control, CI/CD, observability, cloud, ticketing and incident-management products. The operational failure is that these systems do not produce one trustworthy, connected record of what changed, what business service was exposed, whether required controls were satisfied, what likely caused an incident, how recovery was approved, and whether the final evidence is complete.

Engineers reconstruct incidents manually across dashboards, tickets, commits, deployment logs and chat. Change managers often evaluate risk from incomplete form fields. Risk and audit teams later request screenshots and explanations from people who are already busy. The same enterprise may therefore have excellent tools but weak operational truth.

The system must solve the following related problems without becoming another broad monitoring platform:

- Fragmented change context. A code commit, Terraform plan, Kubernetes rollout, ticket and alert are stored in different products with inconsistent identifiers.
- Subjective risk assessment. Change risk is often determined by questionnaires rather than actual service criticality, blast radius, test evidence and operating health.
- Slow incident reconstruction. Teams must manually determine what changed, what dependencies were affected and which observations support each theory.
- Unsafe automation pressure. Generative AI can produce plausible repairs but may lack authority boundaries, evidence fidelity and safe execution controls.
- Manual audit evidence. Proof of approvals, tests, deployments, incidents, remediation and verification is assembled after the fact.
- Weak organizational learning. Prior incidents and effective controls are rarely connected to future change decisions in a structured way.

### 1.2 The product promise

Single-sentence definition
OpsProof is the operational control layer that connects production changes to business services, live system behavior, incidents, remediation decisions and audit evidence.

The product is not a chatbot attached to Datadog. It is a versioned decision and evidence system. AI assists explanation, retrieval and constrained code generation; structured evidence, policy, authorization and customer-controlled execution remain authoritative.

### 1.3 What the system must do

- Observe. Collect normalized metadata from customer systems without requiring those systems to be replaced.
- Understand. Map business services, technical services, resources, dependencies, owners and criticality.
- Assess. Calculate transparent change risk and data-confidence scores using deterministic evidence first.
- Control. Evaluate customer-defined policies and identify missing approvals, tests, rollout protections or rollback readiness.
- Correlate. Rank evidence-backed incident hypotheses and show exactly why each hypothesis is plausible or weak.
- Prepare. Generate a repair branch or pull request, a verification plan and a rollback-of-remediation plan.
- Require human authority. Keep production-impacting actions behind customer approvals and the customer's own delivery pipeline.
- Verify. Measure whether deployment or remediation restored agreed technical and business health indicators.
- Prove. Preserve a tamper-evident, exportable record of inputs, analyses, decisions, actions and outcomes.

### 1.4 Non-negotiable product decisions

| Decision | Required position | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence before eloquence | Every material recommendation must link to source evidence. AI prose without evidence cannot drive action. | Trust is the product. A fluent unsupported answer damages the category. |
| Read-only by default | The customer collector has no production write permission. Initial remediation is delivered through pull requests. | Reduces security objections and protects customer control. |
| No fake confidence | The first release displays evidence strength and data coverage, not uncalibrated probability percentages. | A numerical percentage implies statistical calibration that may not exist. |
| Vendor-neutral layer | Integrate with existing tools; do not build a replacement observability or ITSM platform. | Customer value comes from cross-system context and control. |
| Deterministic controls first | Risk and policy logic must be inspectable and reproducible before machine-learned weighting is introduced. | Regulated buyers need explainability and predictable behavior. |
| Customer pipeline executes | Approved repairs run through the customer's Git/CI/CD process, not an opaque OpsProof shell session. | Preserves segregation of duties, testing and auditability. |
| Metadata minimization | Collect the minimum information needed. Secrets, customer records and raw source code are excluded by default. | Reduces data exposure, procurement friction and breach impact. |
| Operational usefulness over compliance theater | The product helps produce evidence and enforce customer controls; it never promises automatic legal compliance. | Compliance depends on the customer's full control environment and legal interpretation. |
| Unknown remains unknown | Missing or conflicting evidence must remain visible rather than being silently inferred. | Honest uncertainty is safer than false completeness. |
| Reversible automation | The first action path is a reviewable pull request with explicit rollback. | A repair must never be harder to undo than the original change. |

### 1.5 Definition of product success

The product succeeds when customers can prove that OpsProof changes operational behavior, not merely that users log in. Customer-level success must be measured through baseline-versus-post-adoption outcomes and evidence quality.

- A high percentage of production changes is automatically observed and mapped to the correct services.
- Risk assessments are generated before or immediately at deployment and are understandable by engineering and governance users.
- Incident teams reach a defensible first hypothesis faster, with fewer manual tool transitions.
- Accepted remediation recommendations pass the customer's tests and verification checks at a high rate.
- Evidence packets require materially less manual reconstruction and contain fewer missing control artifacts.
- Customer teams trust the system enough to expand from one application group to additional critical services.
- Security teams can clearly explain what the collector reads, what leaves the environment and how every action is authorized.

## 2. Product strategy, positioning and defensibility

### 2.1 Initial category and wedge

The initial category is production change risk and incident evidence for regulated cloud-native environments. The wedge is deliberately narrow: Kubernetes and infrastructure-as-code changes affecting production services, linked to observability signals and incident records.

The long-term category can become an operational risk operating system, but the team must earn that expansion by solving one expensive workflow with exceptional depth. Broad slogans such as “AI for DevOps” are prohibited in product planning and external messaging because they create an undefined product and invite comparison with every observability vendor.

The first product must be able to answer five questions better than the customer's current process:

- What exactly changed?
- Which business and technical services could be affected?
- What evidence makes this change lower or higher risk?
- Did the change likely contribute to the incident?
- What approved action restored service, and can we prove it?

### 2.2 Positioning statement

Positioning
For regulated enterprises that need controlled, explainable production change and incident processes, OpsProof is the vendor-neutral operational control layer that connects changes, dependencies, telemetry, approvals, remediation and evidence. Unlike monitoring-only or ticket-only systems, OpsProof produces one evidence-backed decision record across the existing toolchain.

### 2.3 Customer value hierarchy

| Value layer | Outcome | Primary buyer language |
|---|---|---|
| Operational | Faster diagnosis, fewer change-related incidents, safer rollback and clearer ownership. | Reduce customer impact and engineering interruption. |
| Control | Required reviews, approvals, rollout methods and verification steps are visible and enforceable. | Make production change predictable and governed. |
| Evidence | Audit artifacts are captured automatically at the time of action. | Stop reconstructing proof after the fact. |
| Executive risk | Critical services and risky changes are summarized in business context. | Know where technology change can hurt the company. |
| Learning | Historical incidents improve future risk assessments and recommended controls. | Turn operational history into prevention. |

### 2.4 Competitive boundary

OpsProof must coexist with observability, CI/CD, ITSM, source-control, cloud-security and incident-response products. The team should consistently describe integrations as a strength rather than a temporary limitation.

| Adjacent category | What it typically owns | OpsProof boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Observability/APM | Metrics, traces, logs, service health and alerting. | Uses those signals as evidence; does not reproduce the full telemetry backend. |
| Incident management | On-call routing, escalation, status and coordination. | Creates evidence-backed hypotheses, change linkage, remediation and final evidence. |
| ITSM/change tickets | Workflow records, approvals and enterprise process. | Improves risk context, validates controls and writes results back. |
| CI/CD | Build, test, deploy and rollback execution. | Evaluates risk and policy, prepares changes and observes outcomes. |
| Cloud security/CSPM | Misconfiguration, vulnerability and posture findings. | May use findings as change-risk inputs; does not become a full CSPM. |
| GRC | Control catalogs, assessments and compliance programs. | Produces operational evidence and control outcomes for ingestion by GRC systems. |

### 2.5 Defensibility and moat

- Operational knowledge graph. A customer-specific model linking business functions, services, resources, owners, changes, incidents and outcomes.
- Cross-system evidence. Normalized event history that individual source vendors cannot see.
- Outcome feedback. Knowledge of which changes caused failures, which controls prevented harm and which repairs restored service.
- Embedded workflow. Approvals, evidence and remediation become part of daily engineering and governance work.
- Policy intelligence. Reusable policy templates and mappings refined through customer deployment experience.
- Trust architecture. Data minimization, customer-controlled execution, explainability and verifiable audit history.
- Integration depth. High-fidelity adapters that understand semantic meaning, not shallow API synchronization.
- Historical operating memory. A durable record of the customer's actual system behavior and decisions that becomes more valuable over time.

### 2.6 Build, buy and partner rules

| Area | Default decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Core risk, incident and evidence logic | Build | This is the differentiated intellectual property. |
| Identity federation and SCIM | Buy or use a proven broker behind standards-based interfaces | Enterprise identity is necessary but not differentiating. |
| Cloud object storage, databases and queues | Use managed services in SaaS; support portable equivalents for private edition | Avoid rebuilding infrastructure primitives. |
| Foundation models | Use a provider-abstracted gateway; support customer-approved models | Model quality and policy requirements vary. |
| Policy engine | Use a proven policy runtime where appropriate, with an OpsProof domain layer | Do not invent a general policy language unnecessarily. |
| Observability collection | Adopt OpenTelemetry and native source APIs | Preserves vendor neutrality and standards alignment. |
| PDF/report rendering | Use a controlled rendering service with templates | Consistency and evidentiary integrity matter more than custom layout code. |
| Secrets and key management | Use approved managed or enterprise-grade components | Security plumbing is not a place for novelty. |

## 3. Customers, personas, jobs and buying dynamics

### 3.1 Ideal customer profile

- Regulated financial services, insurance, payments, healthcare, telecommunications or critical digital service provider.
- Material production workloads on Kubernetes, public cloud or hybrid infrastructure.
- Infrastructure as code and formal CI/CD practices are already present.
- Dedicated SRE, platform engineering, DevOps or cloud operations capability exists.
- Formal change, risk, incident and audit processes create measurable administrative burden.
- Recent change-related incidents or audit findings create executive urgency.
- The company wants to improve its current toolchain rather than conduct a wholesale replacement.
- The customer has at least one application group where service ownership and production signals are reasonably identifiable.

### 3.2 Buying committee

| Persona | Primary job | Pain | Success measure | Likely objection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Economic buyer: CIO/CTO | Reduce operational disruption and technology risk. | High incident cost, unclear accountability, fragmented reporting. | Lower change failure and faster recovery across critical services. | “We already own several platforms.” |
| Champion: Head of SRE/Platform | Protect production while increasing delivery speed. | Manual diagnosis, alert overload, incomplete service maps. | Faster evidence-backed diagnosis and safer releases. | “Will this create more process for engineers?” |
| Change manager | Ensure changes are assessed, approved and verified. | Risk questionnaires are subjective and stale. | Consistent controls based on actual technical context. | “Does this integrate with our ITSM workflow?” |
| Incident commander | Coordinate diagnosis, decisions and recovery. | Information is scattered and hypotheses are not tracked. | A clear timeline, owners and ranked evidence. | “Can I trust the system during a crisis?” |
| Technology risk/operational resilience | Understand and govern ICT risk. | Technical records lack business context. | Critical-service evidence and control effectiveness. | “Does this overclaim compliance?” |
| Internal auditor | Test whether controls operated as designed. | Evidence collection is manual and inconsistent. | Searchable, immutable and exportable records. | “Can evidence be altered by admins?” |
| Service owner | Deliver a reliable customer-facing service. | Owns outcomes but cannot see all changes and dependencies. | Clear risk, ownership and post-deployment health. | “Will the score unfairly block my releases?” |
| Security architect | Limit new data and control-plane risk. | Third-party agents and AI can create attack paths. | Least privilege, isolation, auditability and no opaque execution. | “What exactly leaves our environment?” |

### 3.3 Jobs to be done

| When... | I need to... | So that... |
|---|---|---|
| A production change is proposed | Understand blast radius, risk factors and required controls automatically. | I can make a defensible decision without a generic questionnaire. |
| A critical deployment starts | Monitor technical and business health against the approved plan. | Unsafe rollout can be paused or rolled back before broad impact. |
| An incident is declared | See recent relevant changes, dependency paths and evidence-backed hypotheses. | The team spends less time gathering context and more time restoring service. |
| A repair is proposed | Review the exact change, tests, blast radius and verification plan. | I can approve with clear accountability. |
| An audit request arrives | Retrieve the complete record for a service, change, incident or control. | Evidence is delivered without reconstructing events from screenshots. |
| A similar change is proposed later | Use prior incidents and outcomes as risk evidence. | The organization learns rather than repeats the same failure. |

### 3.4 Adoption principles

- Engineering users must receive immediate operational value before governance users ask them to provide additional metadata.
- The product must work with imperfect service catalogs; onboarding cannot depend on a fully mature CMDB.
- Every score must be decomposable into understandable factors and missing-data warnings.
- Recommendations must be optional and reversible; customer policy determines enforcement.
- Audit users receive read-only, scoped views and cannot alter operational records.
- The product must show where data is inferred versus explicitly sourced.
- Workflow should begin in tools users already operate and deep-link into OpsProof when additional context is needed.
- The system must minimize duplicate notifications and avoid becoming another alert source that users learn to ignore.

## 4. Scope boundaries and product sequence gates

### 4.1 Initial product boundary

The initial product must deliver a closed operational loop for a defined application group:

- Ingest change and deployment metadata.
- Map the change to services and dependencies.
- Calculate risk and data confidence.
- Evaluate required customer controls.
- Observe deployment and health signals.
- Correlate incidents with relevant changes.
- Prepare a human-reviewed remediation pull request.
- Verify recovery against explicit indicators.
- Generate a complete evidence packet.
A capability that does not strengthen this loop is not part of the initial product unless a paying design partner requires it and the product council approves the exception.

### 4.2 Capability gates instead of a calendar schedule

The team will progress through evidence-based gates. Work may overlap, but a later gate cannot be treated as production-ready until the preceding trust, security and operability conditions are satisfied.

#### Gate A - Trusted foundation

Objective: establish tenant isolation, identity, canonical events, evidence integrity and platform observability.

Required capabilities

- Tenant, organization, environment, user, role and service entities.
- Enterprise identity federation path, even if initial pilots use managed identities.
- Canonical event envelope and idempotent ingestion.
- Append-only evidence objects with cryptographic hashes.
- Connector registration, health, version and permission inventory.
- Product audit log independent of customer operational evidence.
- Central authorization service and policy enforcement points.
- Platform telemetry, alerting, backup, restoration and incident runbooks.
- Data retention and deletion controls by tenant.
Exit evidence

- Cross-tenant access tests demonstrate isolation.
- Duplicate and out-of-order events do not corrupt state.
- A full customer evidence chain can be reproduced from source events.
- Backup restoration is proven in an isolated environment.
- Every privileged administrative action is attributable and reviewable.

#### Gate B - Service intelligence and change visibility

Objective: construct a useful operational model from GitHub, Kubernetes, Terraform and observability metadata.

Required capabilities

- GitHub App integration for repositories, commits, pull requests, checks and deployments.
- Kubernetes collector for workload, rollout, event and topology metadata.
- Terraform plan/run ingestion from supported execution paths.
- One observability integration: Datadog or Prometheus-based.
- Service catalog with ownership, criticality, environments and dependencies.
- Change normalization and deployment timeline.
- Data-lineage view showing source and freshness for each material field.
Exit evidence

- A representative deployment is automatically linked to the correct repository, commit, Kubernetes workload and service.
- The team can explain every inferred relationship and correct it without rewriting history.
- Missing data is visible and contributes to the data-confidence score.
- Connector permission manifests have passed security review.

#### Gate C - Explainable change control

Objective: assess risk transparently and apply customer-defined controls before or during production change.

Required capabilities

- Deterministic risk model with factor-level evidence.
- Independent data-confidence score.
- Policy evaluation with versioned policy decisions.
- Required-control checklist and approval references.
- Change detail screen and pre-deployment decision record.
- Webhook/API response usable by CI/CD or ITSM workflows.
- Override workflow requiring reason, authority and expiration where applicable.
Exit evidence

- The same evidence and policy version always produce the same result.
- A user can trace each risk factor to source data.
- Policy simulation can show impact before activation.
- Overrides cannot erase the original recommendation or evidence.
- False blocking and missing-detection rates are measured on replay data.

#### Gate D - Evidence-backed incident intelligence

Objective: reduce manual incident reconstruction and produce ranked, testable hypotheses.

Required capabilities

- PagerDuty or equivalent incident intake.
- Incident Room with synchronized timeline.
- Relevant-change retrieval using temporal and topology constraints.
- Evidence-supported hypothesis ranking with contradictory evidence.
- Diagnostic action tracking.
- Human-confirmed cause and contributing-factor capture.
- Post-incident record linked to affected service and future changes.
Exit evidence

- Historical incident replays produce useful top-ranked hypotheses without inventing facts.
- Every hypothesis identifies supporting, missing and contradictory evidence.
- The system remains useful when AI summarization is unavailable.
- Incident commanders can reject or supersede hypotheses while preserving history.

#### Gate E - Controlled remediation

Objective: prepare safe, reviewable repairs without granting OpsProof unrestricted production authority.

Required capabilities

- Repair-plan generation constrained to approved repositories and resource types.
- Branch and pull-request creation through scoped Git permissions.
- Syntax, schema, policy, static and automated test checks.
- Blast-radius reassessment for the proposed repair.
- Explicit approvals and segregation-of-duties checks.
- Verification plan and rollback-of-remediation plan.
- Outcome recording after customer pipeline execution.
Exit evidence

- OpsProof cannot merge or deploy unless a separately approved future capability enables it.
- Generated changes are bounded, reviewable and attributable to model, prompt, evidence and user request.
- Unsafe or ambiguous requests fail closed.
- Failed verification creates a visible escalation path rather than a false success state.

#### Gate F - Evidence Vault and enterprise governance

Objective: make operational evidence searchable, exportable and defensible for risk and audit stakeholders.

Required capabilities

- Evidence packet builder and immutable versions.
- Search by service, change, incident, control, user, time and criticality.
- Read-only auditor workspace.
- Signed exports with manifest and checksums.
- Retention, legal-hold and deletion policy support.
- ServiceNow/GRC export or API integration as customer demand proves.
- Control-effectiveness and evidence-completeness reporting.
Exit evidence

- Exported packets can be independently validated against the manifest.
- Tenant administrators cannot silently alter historical evidence.
- Access to sensitive evidence is scoped and audited.
- A customer can answer a defined audit sample without manual screenshot collection.

#### Gate G - Learning, scale and selective automation

Objective: use outcome history to improve recommendations and introduce narrowly scoped automation safely.

Required capabilities

- Calibrated models trained or tuned only under approved customer-data policy.
- Change-failure propensity and control-effectiveness learning.
- Customer-specific and cross-customer learning kept logically and contractually separate.
- Low-risk automation tiers with explicit policy and revocation.
- Multi-region and private-deployment maturity.
- Advanced executive risk, resilience and trend views.
Exit evidence

- Model improvements outperform deterministic baselines on held-out replay sets.
- Calibration, drift and subgroup performance are measured.
- Automation can be disabled immediately per tenant, service, action and connector.
- No production authority is granted merely because an AI recommendation scores highly.

### 4.3 Initial in-scope capabilities

- GitHub, Kubernetes, Terraform, one observability source and PagerDuty.
- Application/service group onboarding with business criticality and ownership.
- Production changes involving code, Kubernetes configuration and Terraform-managed infrastructure.
- Deterministic risk and data-confidence scoring.
- Customer policy evaluation and control checklist.
- Incident change correlation and evidence-backed hypotheses.
- Git-based repair pull requests for a constrained set of Kubernetes and Terraform failure modes.
- Technical and business-indicator verification.
- Tamper-evident evidence packet and signed export.
- SaaS control plane plus read-only customer collector.

### 4.4 Explicitly out of scope for the initial product

- Building a complete observability, logging, tracing or alerting platform.
- Replacing ITSM, CMDB, incident paging, CI/CD or source-control products.
- General cybersecurity monitoring, vulnerability management or SIEM functionality.
- Unrestricted shell access or autonomous production modification.
- Collecting secrets, production customer records or entire raw source repositories by default.
- Automated legal conclusions such as “DORA compliant” or “audit passed.”
- General-purpose employee chatbot or IT help desk.
- Cloud cost optimization as a standalone module.
- End-user monitoring, synthetic testing and status-page hosting beyond verification inputs.
- Supporting every cloud, deployment engine and observability vendor before product-market evidence exists.
- Machine-learned risk scores that cannot be explained or reproduced.
- Mobile-native applications; responsive web and notification integrations are sufficient initially.

### 4.5 Scope exception rule

A customer request may enter the committed scope only when it satisfies all of the following:

- It strengthens the core closed loop rather than creating an adjacent product.
- At least one paying customer has a material need, and another credible customer can be identified.
- The data, permission and security implications are understood.
- The capability can be supported operationally after delivery.
- Product, architecture, security and design owners approve the decision.
- The opportunity cost and de-scoped work are explicitly recorded.
Every exception requires a written decision record containing customer value, strategic fit, risks, dependencies, acceptance criteria and the work displaced.

## 5. Domain model and system vocabulary

### 5.1 Modeling principles

- A canonical domain object must have a stable OpsProof identifier independent of source-system identifiers.
- Source records remain traceable through immutable references and connector provenance.
- Current state is a projection of versioned events, not a replacement for history.
- Explicit customer data is distinguishable from inferred data.
- Inferences contain method, confidence, source set, timestamp and correction history.
- Business context and technical topology are first-class, not free-text annotations.
- Deletion, retention and legal-hold requirements are modeled at the evidence-object level.
- The domain model must support partial data. Unknown is a valid state.

### 5.2 Core entities

#### Tenant

Represents one contractual customer boundary.

Required fields

- tenant_id
- Legal/display name
- Data residency region
- Subscription edition
- Retention profile
- Encryption key reference
- Identity configuration
- Feature-policy configuration
- Status: provisioning, active, suspended, offboarding, deleted
Rules

- No data object may exist without a tenant boundary.
- Tenant context is derived from verified identity or connector credentials, never from a client-supplied arbitrary header alone.
- Tenant deletion follows a documented, approval-controlled workflow with legal-hold checks.

#### Organization unit

Represents a business division, legal entity, department or technology domain used for access and reporting.

Required fields

- org_unit_id
- Parent organization unit
- Name and description
- Owners
- Cost/business identifiers where supplied
- Access-control tags

#### Environment

Represents development, test, staging, production or another customer-defined operating boundary.

Required fields

- environment_id
- Name and classification
- Production flag
- Region/account/cluster scope
- Data classification
- Change-window policy reference

#### Business service

A customer-facing or internal business capability whose disruption has business impact.

Examples: card authorization, customer claims, online checkout, identity verification.

Required fields

- business_service_id
- Name and description
- Business owner
- Criticality tier
- Recovery objectives when supplied
- Regulatory/materiality tags
- Supported processes and customer groups
- Related technical services
- Source and provenance for each field

#### Technical service

A deployable or operable software capability owned by a team.

Required fields

- technical_service_id
- Name and aliases
- Repository references
- Runtime resources
- Owning team and escalation contact
- Business-service relationships
- Environment-specific instances
- Health indicators and SLO references
- Criticality and data-confidence values

#### Resource

A concrete technical object such as a Kubernetes workload, cluster, namespace, cloud load balancer, database, queue, API gateway or Terraform-managed component.

Required fields

- resource_id
- Type and provider
- Environment and region
- Native source identifier
- Configuration fingerprint
- Parent/child relationships
- Owning technical service when known
- Data sensitivity tag
- First/last observed time

#### Dependency edge

Represents a directed relationship between business services, technical services or resources.

Required fields

- Source entity and target entity
- Relationship type: calls, reads, writes, publishes, subscribes, routes, hosts, depends on, owned by
- Environment scope
- Evidence source
- Explicit versus inferred
- Confidence and freshness
- Valid-from and valid-to timestamps

#### Change

A normalized unit of intended or observed modification.

Change types

- Application code
- Kubernetes manifest or Helm value
- Terraform infrastructure
- Configuration
- Feature flag
- Database/schema, when later supported
- Identity/permission, when later supported
- Emergency manual change, when explicitly recorded
Required fields

- change_id
- Source system and source identifier
- Actor/requester
- Commit, pull request or run references
- Description and structured diff summary
- Affected resources/services
- Environment
- Requested, approved, deployed and completed timestamps
- Planned versus emergency classification
- Test, approval, rollout and rollback evidence
- Risk assessment references
- Status and outcome

#### Deployment

An execution of one or more changes into an environment.

Required fields

- deployment_id
- Change set
- Pipeline/run reference
- Target environment and resources
- Version before and after
- Strategy: full, rolling, canary, blue/green, other
- Start/end times
- Status
- Verification window
- Health observations
- Rollback or forward-fix relationship

#### Risk assessment

An immutable evaluation of a specific change version against a specific data snapshot and model/policy version.

Required fields

- risk_assessment_id
- Change version
- Model version
- Evidence snapshot reference
- Total risk band and score
- Data-confidence score
- Factor results and explanations
- Missing/contradictory data
- Recommended controls
- Created time and triggering event

#### Policy

A versioned customer rule that evaluates facts and requires, recommends, warns, blocks or escalates.

Required fields

- policy_id and version
- Name, purpose and owner
- Scope selectors
- Condition expression
- Decision effect
- Required controls
- Severity
- Effective period
- Test cases
- Draft, simulated, active, retired status

#### Control instance

The application of a policy requirement to a particular change, incident or remediation.

Required fields

- control_instance_id
- Policy version
- Subject object
- Requirement
- Owner/approver role
- Status: required, satisfied, waived, expired, failed
- Evidence references
- Waiver reason, authority and expiration

#### Incident

A disruption or degradation record synchronized from an incident system or declared within OpsProof.

Required fields

- incident_id
- External incident reference
- Severity and status
- Declared/acknowledged/resolved times
- Affected business and technical services
- Incident commander and participants
- Symptoms and health indicators
- Timeline events
- Hypotheses
- Confirmed cause and contributing factors
- Remediation and verification
- Follow-up actions

#### Signal

A bounded observation used as evidence: alert state, metric anomaly, trace characteristic, Kubernetes event, deployment status, test result or business KPI change.

Required fields

- signal_id
- Signal type and source
- Subject resource/service
- Time range
- Normalized value/status
- Source reference and query definition

### 5.3 Source-of-truth rules

| Data | Authoritative source | OpsProof responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Git commit and pull-request state | Source-control platform | Preserve reference, normalize metadata and connect to services/changes. |
| Pipeline execution | CI/CD platform | Observe outcome and connect it to deployment and evidence. |
| Runtime resource state | Kubernetes/cloud source | Capture bounded state and topology with provenance. |
| Alert/metric truth | Observability source | Preserve query and observation; do not silently recalculate source metrics. |
| Incident status and paging | Incident-management system | Synchronize and enrich without creating conflicting authority. |
| Customer policy | OpsProof or designated policy source | Version, simulate, evaluate and preserve decisions. |
| Business criticality | Customer-approved catalog record | Track source, approver and freshness; never infer silently as authoritative. |
| Risk assessment | OpsProof | Make deterministic, versioned and reproducible from evidence snapshot. |
| Evidence integrity | OpsProof | Hash, preserve, authorize and export without silent mutation. |
| Production execution | Customer Git/CI/CD pipeline | Observe and verify; initial product does not become execution authority. |

### 5.4 Identity, versioning and time semantics

- All timestamps are stored in UTC with source timezone and precision where known.
- Event time, collection time and processing time are separate fields.
- Every event has an idempotency key and source sequence/version when available.
- Material objects support optimistic concurrency and immutable historical versions.
- Retractions or corrections create superseding records; they do not delete the original event from the evidence history.
- The UI displays customer-local time while preserving UTC in exports and APIs.
- Correlation logic accounts for clock skew and marks uncertain ordering.
- A risk assessment references the exact evidence and topology versions used.

### 5.5 Data quality dimensions

Every material object may expose:

- Completeness: required fields and expected source coverage present.
- Freshness: age relative to source-specific expectation.
- Consistency: agreement between independent sources.
- Lineage: traceability to source and transformation.
- Authority: explicit customer-approved data versus inference.
- Integrity: hash/checkpoint validation status.
- Availability: source currently reachable and collection current.
These dimensions feed the data-confidence score but remain individually visible.

## 6. End-to-end customer and user journeys

### 6.1 Journey 1 - Tenant and pilot onboarding

#### Entry conditions

- Contract, data-processing terms and security scope are agreed.
- Pilot application group and customer owners are named.
- Customer security approves the collector architecture and permission manifest.
- Success metrics and historical baseline window are defined.

#### Workflow

- Platform administrator provisions the tenant and data-residency region.
- Customer administrator configures identity federation or pilot identities and assigns least-privilege roles.
- Customer creates production and non-production environments.
- Connector wizard generates a permission-specific installation package and preflight checklist.
- Customer installs GitHub App and Kubernetes collector, then connects Terraform, observability and PagerDuty.
- OpsProof validates permissions, connectivity, event freshness and clock synchronization.
- The discovery engine proposes repositories, workloads, services, teams and dependencies.
- Service owners review mappings, criticality, ownership and business-service relationships.
- Historical changes and incidents are replayed in non-enforcing mode.
- Product displays data coverage, unresolved mappings and expected limitations.
- Customer approves pilot policies in simulation mode.
- Readiness review signs off identity, connectors, scope, support, rollback and success measures.

#### Required UX behavior

- The wizard shows exactly what data each permission enables.
- The customer can disable raw logs, source snippets and optional fields independently.
- Discovery suggestions never become authoritative without source attribution and review.
- A readiness dashboard blocks production activation when critical connector or authorization checks fail.

#### Completion evidence

- Tenant configuration export.
- Connector permission manifest and validation report.
- Approved service catalog scope.
- Data coverage baseline.
- Policy simulation report.
- Named operational and security contacts.

### 6.2 Journey 2 - Production change assessment

#### Trigger

A pull request, Terraform plan, release candidate or deployment event enters the configured production path.

#### Workflow

- The connector receives or polls the source event and verifies authenticity.
- OpsProof normalizes the change and associates commits, artifacts, plans and target environment.
- Service graph resolves affected resources, technical services, business services and owners.
- Diff classifiers identify change types and hazards.
- Evidence service collects test status, review status, rollback readiness, deployment strategy, current health and similar history.
- Risk engine produces factor scores and data-confidence dimensions.
- Policy engine evaluates the facts against active customer policy versions.
- OpsProof creates a decision record: risk, uncertainty, required controls, warnings and evidence.
- Result is displayed in OpsProof and returned to the configured CI/CD, Git or ITSM integration.
- A qualified user satisfies controls, requests a waiver or modifies the change.
- Policy is re-evaluated on material evidence changes.
- Final pre-deployment record is sealed when execution begins.

#### Decision states

- Ready: required controls satisfied; this does not mean zero risk.
- Ready with warning: no blocking control, but elevated risk or low confidence is present.
- Action required: one or more required controls are incomplete.
- Blocked by policy: active customer policy prohibits progression.
- Assessment incomplete: required evidence is unavailable or stale.
- Override granted: authorized exception exists with reason and scope.

#### Critical acceptance behavior

- A user can identify why the state changed between two assessments.
- Score changes never overwrite earlier scores.
- A high-confidence low-risk assessment and a low-confidence low-risk assessment look materially different.
- The customer can configure whether OpsProof is advisory or a mandatory CI/CD check.

### 6.3 Journey 3 - Deployment observation and verification

- Deployment start links to the approved change assessment.
- OpsProof records target versions, rollout strategy and baseline health window.
- Verification engine evaluates customer-approved technical and business indicators.
- Rollout stages and health observations are displayed on one timeline.
- Breach of an explicit threshold creates a recommendation or integration signal according to customer policy.
- Customer delivery tooling pauses, rolls back or proceeds; OpsProof records the decision and source.
- Deployment closes only after status and verification window are complete or explicitly waived.
- Outcome feeds historical change and control-effectiveness data.
Initial product constraint: OpsProof may recommend or return a policy signal, but execution remains in the customer's pipeline.

### 6.4 Journey 4 - Live incident investigation

- PagerDuty creates or updates an incident.
- OpsProof maps affected services from incident metadata, alert references and topology.
- Incident Room opens with synchronized source timeline.
- Relevant-change retrieval selects changes within bounded time and dependency paths.
- Correlation engine creates ranked hypotheses.
- AI explanation layer summarizes evidence without changing the underlying score.
- Incident commander assigns diagnostic actions and records observations.
- New evidence automatically recalculates rankings, preserving prior versions.
- Users support, reject, confirm or supersede hypotheses with reasons.
- A remediation plan is selected or created.
- Recovery verification closes the technical incident state.
- Root cause, contributing factors and follow-up actions are reviewed and sealed.

#### Incident Room rule

The first viewport must answer:

- What is affected?
- What changed?
- What evidence is strongest?
- What is uncertain?
- What action is underway?
- Who owns the next decision?

### 6.5 Journey 5 - Human-approved remediation

- User selects a supported hypothesis and requests a remediation proposal.
- System verifies requester authority, supported scope and available evidence.
- Remediation engine chooses an approved strategy: revert, restore prior configuration, scale resource, disable feature, or another bounded template.
- AI may draft code/configuration inside a constrained schema and repository boundary.
- Static checks, policy checks and test suites run.
- Service graph recalculates blast radius for the proposed repair.
- OpsProof creates a branch and pull request with evidence, explanation, tests, verification and rollback-of-remediation plan.
- Required customer reviewers approve through their normal source-control process.
- Customer pipeline merges and deploys.
- OpsProof observes execution and verifies recovery indicators.
- Outcome is recorded as successful, partially successful, failed or inconclusive.
- Failed or inconclusive verification triggers escalation; it never silently closes the incident.

### 6.6 Journey 6 - Evidence and audit request

- Authorized auditor selects scope: service, control, change, incident, time range or sample.
- OpsProof shows available evidence, completeness and access restrictions.
- User previews packet contents and applies an approved redaction profile where required.
- System builds an immutable packet version with manifest, lineage and checksums.
- Authorized approver releases the packet.
- Export is generated in human-readable PDF plus machine-readable JSON/CSV as configured.
- Recipient can validate manifest and signature/checkpoint.
- Every view, export and redaction action is logged.

### 6.7 Journey 7 - Emergency change

- Emergency classification never bypasses evidence collection.
- Customer policy determines which pre-change controls may be deferred.
- The requester must record reason, impact, authority and intended rollback.
- OpsProof prominently marks reduced evidence or waived controls.
- Retrospective review becomes a tracked control with owner and due condition.
- The emergency designation, waiver and retrospective evidence remain in the permanent record.
- Repeated emergency changes for the same service appear as an executive and service-owner signal.

### 6.8 Journey state model

The workflow intentionally separates assessment, authorization, execution and verification. No single AI output or user click should collapse those responsibilities.

## 8. Change-risk, incident-correlation and policy logic

### 8.1 Design goals

The first models must be useful, transparent and deterministic. They should help qualified humans reason consistently; they must not manufacture statistical precision.

The product displays two separate concepts:

- Risk score: the modeled potential that the proposed change could cause material operational harm given known facts.
- Data-confidence score: the quality, completeness, freshness and consistency of evidence supporting that assessment.
A low risk score with low confidence is not equivalent to a low risk score with high confidence.

### 8.2 Change-risk score version 1

The default score is 0-100. Each factor has a maximum weight. A factor contributes according to typed rules defined by model version. Customer policies may require additional controls but do not silently change the core score.

| Factor | Maximum points | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Business/service criticality | 15 | Criticality tier, material process, recovery objective. |
| Blast radius | 15 | Downstream services, regions, tenants, shared infrastructure. |
| Change hazard | 12 | Resource reduction, network route, security boundary, stateful change, broad config. |
| Historical similarity | 10 | Prior incidents from similar change/resource/service. |
| Test deficiency | 10 | Missing, failed, stale or non-representative tests. |
| Rollback deficiency | 10 | No tested rollback, irreversible migration, unavailable prior artifact. |
| Deployment safety deficiency | 8 | Full rollout, no canary, no progressive delivery, no automatic stop signal. |
| Current service health | 8 | Existing error, saturation, active incident or SLO burn. |
| Temporal/context risk | 5 | Change freeze, high-traffic period, limited support coverage, dependency event. |
| Governance gap | 4 | Missing owner, reviewer, separation of duties or required record. |
| Novelty | 3 | New component, first-time pattern, uncommon operator or insufficient history. |
| Total | 100 |  |

#### Default bands

- 0-24: Low - lower modeled risk based on available evidence; never labeled “safe.”
- 25-49: Moderate - proceed only with normal required controls and explicit review of notable factors.
- 50-74: High - elevated controls and staged rollout normally expected.
- 75-100: Critical - substantial risk; policy should normally block or require exceptional authority and controls.

#### Example factor rules

Criticality

- Tier 4/non-production: 0-2 points.
- Tier 3/internal non-critical: 3-6 points.
- Tier 2/material customer or employee service: 7-11 points.
- Tier 1/critical business service: 12-15 points.
Blast radius

- One isolated non-production resource: 0 points.
- One production service with no known downstream critical dependency: 3-5 points.
- Multiple production services or shared infrastructure: 6-10 points.
- Multi-region, identity, payment, network or broadly shared control plane: 11-15 points.
Change hazard

Typed classifiers identify hazards such as:

- Resource limit reduction.
- Replica reduction.
- Health-check change.
- Network/security group or route change.
- Identity, permission or secret-reference change.
- Data-store or stateful-resource change.
- Terraform destroy/replace action.
- Broad wildcard or selector expansion.
- Version downgrade or major-version upgrade.
- Configuration removal or default behavior change.
The classifier returns evidence and severity; unknown syntax does not receive a benign default.

### 8.3 Data-confidence score version 1

The score is 0-100 and is presented with dimension-level status.

| Dimension | Weight | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Source coverage | 25 | Expected Git, pipeline, runtime, observability and incident sources connected. |
| Mapping completeness | 20 | Change-to-resource, resource-to-service and service-to-business mappings. |
| Freshness | 15 | Source lag within expected threshold. |
| Consistency | 15 | Source identifiers, timestamps and states agree. |
| Test/control evidence | 10 | Required test and approval records are accessible. |
| Topology confidence | 10 | Dependency edges have adequate evidence and recency. |
| Integrity/lineage | 5 | Evidence hashes and provenance validate. |
| Total | 100 |  |

#### Confidence bands

- 85-100: Strong coverage
- 65-84: Adequate with limitations
- 40-64: Material gaps
- 0-39: Assessment incomplete
The UI must show the missing dimensions and how a user could improve them. Data confidence is not a judgment of the engineer or team.

### 8.4 Risk calculation contract

Each assessment input contains:

- Change snapshot and diff summary.
- Topology snapshot.
- Service criticality and ownership snapshot.
- Current health snapshot.
- Test, review, rollout and rollback evidence.
- Historical similarity results.
- Active model and policy versions.
- Source health and freshness.
Each output contains:

- Total score and band.
- Factor points and maximums.
- Plain-language deterministic explanations.
- Evidence references.
- Missing and contradictory evidence.
- Data-confidence score and dimensions.
- Recommended controls.
- Calculation trace and version.

### 8.5 Incident hypothesis evidence score

The incident engine ranks hypotheses using bounded evidence. It is not a legal or scientific claim of causation.

| Component | Maximum points | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal alignment | 20 | Did the candidate change precede symptoms within a plausible window? |
| Topology/path relevance | 20 | Is the changed resource on a credible dependency path to the affected service? |
| Mechanism-symptom match | 20 | Can the change mechanism explain the observed symptom? |
| Corroborating telemetry | 15 | Do metrics/events/traces support the mechanism? |
| Change hazard strength | 10 | Did the diff contain a relevant high-risk pattern? |
| Historical similarity | 10 | Has a similar change caused a similar incident? |
| Contextual consistency | 5 | Do environment, region, version and scope align? |
| Contradictory evidence penalty | Up to -25 | What evidence argues against the hypothesis? |

#### Evidence-strength labels

- 80-100: Strongly supported
- 60-79: Supported
- 40-59: Plausible
- 20-39: Weak
- 0-19: Unlikely
The product should use these labels before displaying calibrated probabilities. A later probability display requires validation and calibration evidence.

### 8.6 Relevant-change retrieval

Candidate changes should be selected through a staged filter:

- Same tenant and environment.
- Time window relative to symptom onset, with source clock-skew allowance.
- Directly affected resource or service.
- Upstream/downstream dependency path within configured depth.
- Shared infrastructure or control-plane relation.
- Mechanism compatibility with observed symptoms.
- Historical similarity and known recurring failure mode.
The engine must retain a record of excluded candidates and reasons for debugging and evaluation, while the standard UI shows only useful candidates.

### 8.7 Policy decision hierarchy

A policy outcome is separate from risk:

- System safety constraints - cannot be overridden by tenant policy, such as prohibition on unrestricted execution in the initial product.
- Contract/edition constraints - capabilities enabled for the tenant.
- Customer mandatory policies - approved production controls.
- Customer advisory policies - warnings and recommendations.
- User decision - approve, reject, defer, modify or request waiver within authority.
Conflicting customer policies resolve by explicit priority and “most restrictive wins” as the safe default. Conflicts are surfaced to policy administrators.

### 8.8 Controls catalog

Initial reusable controls include:

- Named service owner approval.
- Independent reviewer approval.
- Security approval.
- Change-manager approval.
- Required automated test suite.
- Required load/performance test.
- Terraform plan review.
- Verified rollback artifact.
- Canary or progressive rollout.
- Defined stop/rollback thresholds.
- Change-window requirement.
- Active-incident prohibition.
- Backup/restore evidence for stateful change.
- Post-deployment monitoring window.
- Emergency retrospective review.
- Separation of requester, approver and deployer.
Each control has typed evidence requirements; free-text “done” is insufficient unless the control explicitly permits attestation.

### 8.9 Overrides and waivers

- Waivers are policy-governed objects, not comments.
- A waiver identifies subject, control, reason, authority, scope and expiration.
- The requester cannot approve their own waiver unless the customer's policy explicitly permits it and the exception is visible.
- The original risk and policy decision remain unchanged.
- The product shows whether the waiver was used and the outcome.
- Repeated waivers by service/control appear in governance reporting.
- An expired waiver cannot be reused.

### 8.10 Model and policy version management

- Every version has owner, purpose, change summary, tests and approval.
- New model versions run in shadow mode against historical and live events.
- The team compares score distributions, false block/miss proxies, data requirements and customer impact.
- Historical assessments retain their original version.
- Recalculation creates a new comparative assessment, clearly marked as retrospective.
- Rollback to a prior model/policy version is supported.
- Tenant-specific customization is bounded to prevent unmaintainable forks.

## 9. AI architecture, safety model and evaluation

### 9.1 AI role in the product

AI is an assistive subsystem inside a controlled evidence and authorization platform. It is not the source of truth, policy authority, approver or production executor.

#### Approved AI use cases

- Summarize a bounded change, incident timeline or evidence packet.
- Translate structured risk factors into role-appropriate explanations.
- Retrieve and summarize relevant runbooks, prior incidents and internal documentation.
- Propose diagnostic steps tied to a hypothesis.
- Draft constrained Kubernetes, Helm or Terraform remediation changes.
- Draft tests, verification plans and rollback-of-remediation plans.
- Draft post-incident reviews from approved timeline and findings.
- Convert a natural-language policy request into a typed draft for human review.
- Classify unstructured ticket or commit text into non-authoritative tags.
- Assist users in querying the evidence graph through a controlled semantic interface.

#### Prohibited AI use cases in the initial product

- Directly modify, merge or deploy to production.
- Grant access, approve changes or waive controls.
- Make legal conclusions or certify compliance.
- Invent missing facts or silently convert inference into source evidence.
- Execute arbitrary shell commands, network calls or code supplied by model output.
- Retrieve data outside the user's authorization scope.
- Train shared models on customer data by default.
- Generate employee blame, performance or disciplinary rankings.
- Replace deterministic policy evaluation with free-form model judgment.
- Use model confidence language as a substitute for calibrated evidence strength.

### 9.2 AI system architecture

All model requests pass through a central AI Gateway. No application service calls an external model provider directly.

#### AI Gateway responsibilities

- Authenticate tenant, actor, feature and purpose.
- Enforce model/provider allowlists and tenant policy.
- Resolve data residency and private-model routing.
- Apply authorization before retrieval.
- Redact or tokenize configured sensitive fields.
- Build prompts from versioned templates and typed evidence.
- Mark every retrieved item with provenance and trust level.
- Enforce output schema and token/size limits.
- Run content, secret and code-safety checks.
- Log request metadata, template version, model version, retrieval references and result disposition.
- Track cost, latency, error, refusal and fallback behavior.
- Support deterministic/non-AI fallback.
- Prevent provider retention/training where contractually supported and required.

#### AI request record

Every material AI output must be reproducible to the practical limit and contain:

- Tenant and authorized actor.
- Feature and stated purpose.
- Prompt-template version.
- Model/provider and version identifier.
- Model parameters.
- Evidence/retrieval item identifiers and content hashes.
- Redaction/transformation record.
- Output schema version.
- Raw structured output or secure reference.
- Validation, safety and policy results.
- User acceptance, edit, rejection or downstream use.
- Request and response timestamps and latency.

### 9.3 Retrieval-augmented generation

#### Approved source classes

- Customer-approved runbooks.
- Service documentation.
- Prior incidents and remediations within authorized tenant scope.
- Current structured evidence.
- Approved policy/control descriptions.
- Product documentation.

#### Retrieval controls

- Authorization is applied before query and again before result assembly.
- Tenant, organization, environment and service filters are mandatory.
- Retrieval results carry source, version, timestamp, owner and trust classification.
- Low-trust unstructured content is clearly separated from authoritative structured evidence.
- The model may cite retrieved content but cannot promote it to source evidence automatically.
- Content expiration and deletion propagate to indexes.
- Embeddings are tenant-isolated or cryptographically/logically partitioned.
- Sensitive raw content is excluded unless explicitly enabled.

### 9.4 Prompt-injection and untrusted-content model

Commit messages, pull-request descriptions, tickets, logs, alerts, runbooks and documents may contain malicious or accidental instructions. The system treats all retrieved customer content as data, never as trusted instructions.

#### Required controls

- Separate system/developer instructions from retrieved content through structured message roles and delimiters.
- Label every content item with trust class and source.
- Strip or neutralize unsupported active content and links.
- Never allow retrieved content to select tools, expand authorization or change system policy.
- Use an allowlisted tool router based on the feature, not model choice.
- Require typed arguments and server-side authorization for every tool call.
- Prohibit model-created arbitrary URLs, shell commands or SQL from executing directly.
- Scan outputs for secret-like material, instructions to bypass controls and scope expansion.
- Test multilingual, encoded, indirect and cross-document injection patterns.
- Record injection detections without exposing sensitive payloads to broad users.

### 9.5 Structured output contracts

AI outputs that influence product state must validate against strict schemas.

#### Example diagnostic suggestion contract

{
  "hypothesis_id": "hyp_01J...",
  "objective": "Determine whether pod terminations are caused by memory pressure",
  "steps": [
    {
      "type": "query_existing_evidence",
      "source": "kubernetes_events",
      "parameters": {
        "reason": "OOMKilled",
        "service_id": "svc_payments_api",
        "window": "incident"
      },
      "expected_supporting_result": "OOMKilled events begin after deployment",
      "expected_refuting_result": "No memory-related terminations in affected workloads"
    }
  ],
  "limitations": ["Raw container logs are not enabled for this tenant"]
}

The application rejects unknown tool types, unauthorized parameters, missing evidence links and oversized outputs.

### 9.6 Remediation-generation safety pipeline

A remediation proposal proceeds through all applicable stages:

- Intent validation: confirm the user request and supported remediation goal.
- Scope resolution: identify allowed tenant, environment, repository, path, service and resource types.
- Evidence sufficiency: require a supported hypothesis or explicit human-selected strategy.
- Plan generation: create a typed, human-readable change plan before code generation.
- Template/model generation: produce the smallest bounded diff.
- Secret and prohibited-content scan.
- Syntax and schema validation: Kubernetes/OpenAPI, Helm, HCL/Terraform and repository rules.
- Static analysis: lint, policy-as-code and security checks.
- Plan/test execution: customer-approved sandbox or CI checks.
- Blast-radius and risk reassessment.
- Human review: required customer reviewers in source control.
- Customer pipeline execution.
- Verification and outcome classification.
- Evidence sealing and learning feedback.
Failure at any stage stops progression and records the reason. A user may manually continue outside OpsProof, but the system marks the action as external and verifies what actually deployed.

### 9.7 Model/provider strategy

- Use a provider-neutral interface with capability metadata.
- Route tasks by sensitivity, context size, structured-output reliability, code capability, latency and cost.
- Support customer-approved hosted models and private/self-hosted models for enterprise editions.
- Maintain a tested fallback model only where tenant policy permits.
- Pin model versions for controlled features; do not accept silent provider upgrades.
- Revalidate before changing a model, prompt template, retrieval strategy or safety layer.
- Separate embeddings, classifiers and generative models in inventory and risk review.
- Maintain an AI bill of materials containing models, providers, versions, purposes, data classes and owners.

### 9.8 AI evaluation framework

#### Evaluation layers

- Schema and safety validity - valid JSON, no secrets, no unauthorized actions, no unsupported claims.
- Evidence fidelity - every factual statement supported by supplied evidence; citations point to the correct item.
- Task correctness - summary, classification, diagnostic or code outcome is correct.
- Operational usefulness - qualified users rate the output actionable and clear.
- Calibration and uncertainty - system expresses limitations appropriately.
- Robustness - handles missing, conflicting, noisy and adversarial content.
- Fairness and non-blame - avoids unsupported attribution to individuals or teams.
- Performance and economics - latency, availability and cost within feature budget.

#### Required evaluation datasets

- Synthetic canonical incidents with known causes.
- De-identified or contractually approved historical incident replays.
- Hard negatives: temporally close but unrelated changes.
- Multiple simultaneous changes.
- Incomplete topology and missing telemetry.
- Clock skew and out-of-order events.
- Contradictory signals.
- Prompt-injection payloads in tickets, commits, logs and runbooks.
- Multi-language operational text.
- Unsupported remediation requests.
- Valid and invalid Kubernetes/Terraform diffs.

#### Evaluation metrics

- Evidence citation precision and recall.
- Unsupported factual-claim rate.
- Top-k hypothesis usefulness and confirmed-cause rank.
- Diagnostic-step correctness.
- Remediation compile/validate/test pass rate.
- Unsafe suggestion rate.
- Secret leakage rate.
- Human acceptance, edit and rejection rates.
- Time saved versus manual baseline.
- Model refusal appropriateness.
- Latency and cost per successful outcome.

#### Release rule

No AI capability reaches production because a demo looks impressive. It reaches production only after passing feature-specific thresholds, adversarial tests, security review, human-factors review and rollback readiness.

### 9.9 Human factors and automation-bias controls

- Show evidence before generated narrative.
- Use neutral language such as “supported by” rather than “the AI knows.”
- Make rejection and correction easy and non-punitive.
- Never visually imply that accepting the top recommendation is mandatory.
- Present important contradictory evidence without requiring expansion.
- Require users to confirm the intended service/environment before remediation.
- Track overreliance indicators, such as repeated acceptance without opening evidence, only as product-safety telemetry and not employee performance scoring.
- Provide training that explains limitations and escalation.
- Preserve manual workflows when AI is unavailable.

### 9.10 AI incident response

AI-specific incidents include:

- Cross-tenant or unauthorized retrieval.
- Sensitive-data disclosure.
- Systematic unsupported claims.
- Unsafe remediation suggestion.
- Prompt-injection bypass.
- Provider/model behavior change.
- Material evaluation regression.
- Logging or evidence gap.
Response must support feature kill switch, model rollback, tenant notification assessment, affected-output identification, evidence preservation and post-incident corrective action.

## 10. Technical architecture and deployment topology

### 10.1 Architecture principles

- Control plane, not data exhaust warehouse. Ingest bounded operational metadata and evidence required for decisions.
- Event-driven and replayable. Source events are normalized once and projections can be rebuilt.
- Least privilege. Each component receives only required access.
- Tenant isolation by construction. Authorization and partitioning are embedded in every layer.
- Portable core. Managed cloud services may be used, but interfaces support private deployment equivalents.
- Deterministic core, assistive AI. Risk, policy and evidence remain available without a model provider.
- Observable by default. Every service exports standardized telemetry and business events.
- Fail visibly. Stale, partial or unavailable sources reduce confidence and produce clear states.
- No hidden production path. Customer execution remains visible in Git/CI/CD.
- Schema governance. Events and APIs are versioned, tested and backward compatible.

### 10.6 Event-processing architecture

#### Event flow

- Source connector produces authenticated event.
- Ingestion validates and writes durable event before acknowledging.
- Normalizer emits canonical event with lineage.
- Entity resolver links or proposes entities.
- Domain services update versioned projections.
- Risk/policy/incident services react to relevant facts.
- Evidence service records material decisions and outputs.
- UI receives change notifications via event subscription, websocket or polling fallback.

#### Delivery semantics

- Assume at-least-once delivery.
- Consumers are idempotent.
- Ordering is guaranteed only within a defined source/entity partition where possible.
- Late events trigger new projections/assessment versions; they do not mutate history.
- Dead-letter events are visible, retryable and tenant-scoped.
- Schema registry enforces compatibility.

### 10.7 Multi-tenancy and isolation

#### SaaS default

- Tenant identifier is present in every database key and event partition.
- PostgreSQL row-level security or equivalent defense is enabled in addition to application authorization.
- Per-tenant envelope-encryption keys or key hierarchy.
- Object-store prefixes/buckets and access policies enforce isolation.
- Search indexes are per tenant or use rigorously filtered partitions with tested authorization.
- Cache keys include tenant and authorization scope.
- Queue topics/partitions prevent consumer scope confusion.
- AI retrieval and vector stores are tenant-isolated.
- Support tools require time-bounded delegated access.

#### High-isolation option

- Dedicated database, event namespace, object storage and compute boundary.
- Customer-specific keys and network controls.
- Independent maintenance windows and model routing.
- Shared control-management plane may contain only minimal non-sensitive tenancy metadata.

### 10.8 Deployment editions

#### Managed SaaS with customer collector

- EU data-region default for European customers.
- Shared or high-isolation control plane.
- Read-only collector and source APIs.
- Fastest standard deployment and full managed operations.

#### Single-tenant managed cloud

- Dedicated tenant runtime and data services in OpsProof-managed account/subscription.
- Customer-specific network connectivity and keys where agreed.
- Appropriate for strict isolation and procurement requirements.

#### Customer private cloud

- OpsProof services deployed into customer-owned cloud account.
- Customer owns infrastructure boundary; OpsProof operates through delegated controls.
- Requires portable dependencies, documented upgrades and support telemetry controls.

#### On-premises/air-gapped exception

- Supported only after architecture and commercial justification.
- Requires offline artifact delivery, local model option, license controls, update process and diagnostic export.
- Must not fork core product behavior.

### 10.9 Network architecture

- Public endpoints are limited to web/API/ingestion surfaces behind managed protection.
- Private service-to-service traffic uses authenticated workload identity and encryption.
- Administrative interfaces are isolated from customer traffic.
- Production data services are not directly internet-accessible.
- Egress is allowlisted where practical, especially AI providers and notification services.
- Customer collector establishes outbound connections only.
- Private connectivity options include PrivateLink/Private Service Connect or customer VPN patterns.
- DNS, certificates and endpoint ownership are inventoried and monitored.

### 10.11 Scalability and backpressure

- Apply per-tenant and per-connector quotas based on events, payload size and concurrency.
- Separate high-volume telemetry metadata from control/decision events.
- Preserve control events before low-priority enrichment.
- Use bounded graph traversal and query limits.
- Partition by tenant and stable entity where ordering matters.
- Support selective historical backfill without overwhelming live processing.
- Rate-limit expensive AI and evidence-packet operations independently.
- Expose customer-visible throttling and data-loss status.

### 10.12 Data storage lifecycle

- Transactional metadata: PostgreSQL with point-in-time recovery.
- Canonical event/evidence payloads: immutable object storage plus indexed metadata.
- Search documents: rebuildable derivative.
- Cache: disposable derivative.
- Analytics: purpose-limited derivative with retention.
- Vector embeddings: derivative tied to source lifecycle and authorization.
- Customer deletion propagates through primary and derivative stores, backups according to policy, and provider systems.

### 10.13 Platform observability

All services must emit OpenTelemetry-compatible:

- Request and event traces with tenant-safe attributes.
- RED metrics: rate, errors and duration.
- Queue lag, dead letters and replay metrics.
- Connector freshness and source coverage.
- Authorization decisions and denial trends without sensitive payloads.
- AI latency, cost, failure and safety disposition.
- Evidence integrity and packet-generation metrics.
- Business events for assessment, incident and remediation outcomes.
Logs must avoid secrets, raw customer content and unrestricted identifiers. Debug logging in production is time-bounded and authorized.

### 10.14 Disaster recovery and regional failure

- Infrastructure and application configuration are reproducible from reviewed code.
- Database restoration and object integrity are tested.
- Event streams and evidence objects support regional replication consistent with residency commitments.
- DNS/traffic failover does not bypass tenant-region policy.
- AI provider failure degrades assistive features but not deterministic risk, policy, evidence or manual incident functions.
- Connector buffering protects bounded event continuity.
- Customer-facing status explains affected capabilities and data confidence.

## 11. Connector and integration specifications

### 11.1 Common connector contract

Every connector must implement:

- Supported source versions and rate limits.
- Webhook, streaming or polling strategy.
- Initial backfill and incremental cursor.
- Canonical events produced.
- Idempotency and ordering behavior.

- Uninstall, credential revocation and deletion behavior.

### 11.2 GitHub connector - Priority 0

#### Authentication

- GitHub App preferred over personal access token.
- Installation scoped to selected organizations and repositories.
- Webhook signature validation and replay protection.
- Private key stored through secure key management.

#### Minimum read scope

- Repository metadata.
- Commits and changed-file metadata.
- Pull requests, reviews and status checks.
- Deployments and environments where used.
- Workflow runs/check results as required.
- Teams/users only to the minimum needed for ownership and approval context.

#### Optional write scope

- Create branch, commit and pull request for approved remediation repositories.

- Pull request opened/synchronized/reviewed/merged.
- Push/commit.
- Check suite/run.
- Deployment and deployment status.
- Workflow run/job.
- Repository/team changes relevant to mapping.

- Link commit SHA, artifact/version, pull request and deployment.

- Signature and replay attacks rejected.
- Repository uninstall/reinstall handled without cross-repository data leakage.

- Optional PR write cannot affect repositories outside allowlist.

### 12.7 Webhook delivery

- Each destination has a unique signing secret or asymmetric key.
- Payload contains event ID, event type, attempt and timestamp.
- Receiver validates timestamp window and signature over raw body.
- OpsProof retries with exponential backoff and jitter.
- Customer can replay a bounded event through the UI/API.
- Delivery history and response status are visible.
- Sensitive fields are excluded from webhook payload unless explicitly configured.
- Ordering is not guaranteed; consumers use event time and version.

### 12.8 Schema governance

- Schema definitions live in a versioned contracts package.
- Producers and consumers run compatibility tests in CI.
- Additive optional fields are preferred.
- Field semantics cannot change within a version.
- Deprecated fields have published replacement and removal policy.
- Examples and fixture events are part of the contract.
- Personally identifiable or sensitive fields require classification review before introduction.
- AI prompts consume typed domain objects, not ad hoc database records.

### 12.9 Idempotency and concurrency

- Mutating external APIs accept an Idempotency-Key scoped to tenant, actor and operation.
- Create operations return the original resource on safe replay.
- Versioned resources use ETag/If-Match or version fields.
- Approval and waiver decisions reject stale state.
- Event ingestion uses source-derived idempotency keys when available.
- Background jobs use durable leases and idempotent checkpoints.

## 13. Security, privacy, compliance and operational resilience

### 13.1 Security objectives

- Prevent cross-tenant and unauthorized data access.
- Minimize customer data and production permissions.
- Preserve integrity and provenance of evidence and decisions.
- Prevent AI or connector content from expanding authority.
- Secure software supply chain and deployment process.
- Detect, contain and learn from security incidents.
- Support customer due diligence with verifiable documentation.
- Make secure behavior the default rather than a premium configuration.

### 13.2 Threat model summary

| Threat | Example | Primary controls |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-tenant access | Incorrect query filter exposes another customer. | Tenant-bound identity, row-level security, per-tenant partitions/keys, negative authorization tests. |
| Connector compromise | Stolen collector identity sends false events or exfiltrates metadata. | mTLS/workload identity, scoped permissions, signed images, rotation, anomaly detection, revocation. |
| Evidence tampering | Admin edits a past approval or incident record. | Append-only events, content hashes, signed checkpoints, independent audit log, immutable export. |
| Prompt injection | Ticket tells model to ignore policy and expose secrets. | Untrusted-content isolation, allowlisted tools, typed calls, server-side auth, adversarial testing. |
| Generated-code risk | Model proposes destructive Terraform. | Scope allowlist, static/policy checks, plan review, human approval, customer pipeline, verification. |
| Credential leakage | Secrets appear in logs, plan data or prompts. | Local redaction, secret scanning, no Secrets access, log policy, encrypted references. |
| Supply-chain compromise | Malicious dependency or collector image. | Pinned dependencies, SBOM, signing, provenance, scanning, protected builds and release approvals. |
| Insider abuse | Employee browses customer incidents without need. | No standing access, just-in-time support grants, monitoring, segregation of duties and review. |
| Denial of service | Event flood overwhelms ingestion or tenant queries. | Quotas, backpressure, WAF, bounded queries, circuit breakers, workload isolation. |
| Data deletion failure | Customer data persists in indexes/backups after offboarding. | Lifecycle inventory, deletion workflow, derivative tracking, backup expiry and verification report. |
| Identity compromise | Stolen administrator session changes policy. | Federation, MFA/step-up, session controls, anomaly alerts, dual approval for sensitive settings. |
| Model/provider breach | Third-party model retains sensitive context. | Data routing policy, minimization, contractual controls, private model option, provider isolation. |

A detailed threat model is required for each connector, AI tool, privileged workflow and deployment edition.

### 13.3 Identity and access management

#### Authentication

- Enterprise federation through OIDC or SAML.
- MFA enforced through identity provider; step-up for sensitive actions.
- SCIM for lifecycle automation.
- Short-lived sessions and refresh-token protections.
- Service accounts use scoped, expiring credentials or workload identity.
- Local break-glass accounts are few, hardware-protected and monitored.

#### Default roles

| Role | Core permissions |
|---|---|
| Tenant administrator | Tenant settings, identity, connectors and retention; no automatic right to alter evidence. |
| Security administrator | Access review, connector permission review, security settings and audit log. |
| Policy author | Draft and test policy; cannot self-activate unless separately authorized. |
| Policy approver | Review and activate policy. |
| Platform/SRE engineer | View assigned services, changes/incidents, propose remediation. |
| Service owner | Review risk and approve within assigned services. |
| Incident commander | Manage incident timeline, actions, hypotheses and recovery status. |
| Remediation approver | Approve remediation plan within defined scope. |
| Auditor | Read scoped evidence and create authorized packets; no operational mutation. |
| Executive viewer | Aggregated risk/outcome reporting; limited technical sensitive detail. |
| Support delegate | Time-bounded customer-approved diagnostic access. |

#### Authorization model

Use role-based access for broad job function and attribute/resource-based rules for tenant, organization, service, environment, data class and action. Authorization is enforced server-side for every object and search result.

#### Sensitive actions requiring additional controls

- Activate/retire mandatory policy.
- Grant privileged role.
- Enable optional write connector permission.
- Create or release an evidence export containing restricted data.
- Grant customer support access.
- Change retention/legal-hold settings.
- Change AI provider/data policy.
- Delete tenant data.

### 13.4 Cryptography and key management

- TLS 1.2+ with modern configuration; TLS 1.3 preferred.
- Encryption at rest for databases, event stores, object stores, indexes and backups.
- Envelope encryption with managed KMS/HSM-backed keys.
- Separate key hierarchy by environment and tenant where supported.
- Key rotation, revocation and access logging.
- Connector secrets stored only in approved secrets manager; application databases keep references.
- Sensitive exports optionally encrypted to recipient-controlled key.
- Cryptographic algorithms and libraries follow approved security standards and inventory.

### 13.5 Evidence integrity design

OpsProof requires tamper evidence, not marketing theater.

#### Required approach

- Compute content hash for every evidence item over canonical representation.
- Preserve source authenticity information such as webhook signature validation.
- Link material evidence/decision events through a hash chain within bounded streams or batches.
- Create signed periodic checkpoints stored in an independently protected location.
- Use object-store versioning and retention lock/WORM capability where contractually required.
- Include item hashes, packet manifest and checkpoint/signature in exports.
- Validate integrity during packet build and on demand.
- Record any source correction as a new superseding item.
A blockchain is not required. The design goal is independently verifiable integrity with lower complexity and clear ownership.

### 13.6 Data classification and minimization

#### Default collected data

- Source identifiers and metadata.
- Commit/diff structural metadata for supported files.
- Deployment and runtime resource metadata.
- Aggregated metric/alert observations.
- Incident and approval metadata.
- Service ownership and business criticality.
- Generated decisions, recommendations and evidence.

#### Excluded by default

- Secrets and credentials.
- Production customer records.
- Full raw application logs.
- Full source repositories.
- Pod filesystem or process data.
- Message/email content unrelated to incidents.
- Employee sensitive data beyond necessary identity and role.

#### Privacy requirements

- Purpose and legal basis are documented with the customer.
- Data subject and deletion workflows are supported where applicable.
- Search indexes, embeddings and backups are included in lifecycle inventory.
- Data residency and subprocessors are transparent.
- Production data is not used for unrelated model training.
- Product analytics avoid customer payloads and unnecessary personal data.
- Retention defaults are conservative and configurable.

### 13.7 Secure software development lifecycle

#### Repository and code controls

- Protected main branch and required reviews.
- Code owners for security-sensitive areas.
- Signed commits/tags where practical.
- Secret scanning and pre-commit protections.
- Static analysis, dependency and container scanning.
- Infrastructure and policy-as-code scanning.
- Threat model/security review for material capabilities.
- No production credentials in developer environments.

#### Build and release controls

- Isolated, ephemeral build workers where practical.
- Pinned dependencies and locked build definitions.
- Software bill of materials for platform and collector artifacts.
- Signed images/packages and verifiable provenance.
- Artifact promotion rather than rebuild between environments.
- Separate approval for production release.
- Progressive deployment and automatic rollback conditions.
- Emergency release procedure with retrospective review.

#### Vulnerability management

- Asset and dependency inventory.
- Severity and exploitability-based triage.
- Customer notification and patch process.
- Coordinated vulnerability disclosure channel.
- Penetration testing before broad enterprise launch and after material architecture change.
- Connector and collector included in scope.

### 13.8 Logging and security monitoring

Security-relevant events include:

- Authentication success/failure and session anomalies.
- Role, policy, connector and retention changes.
- Support and break-glass access.
- Data export, deletion and legal-hold actions.
- Authorization denials and suspicious enumeration.
- Connector credential or permission changes.
- AI safety blocks and cross-scope attempts.
- Evidence integrity failures.
- Release and privileged infrastructure changes.
Logs are protected from alteration, time-synchronized, retained according to policy and monitored with response runbooks. Logging must not create a second sensitive-data leak.

### 13.9 Regulatory and standards alignment

#### DORA support position

For covered EU financial entities, DORA and its associated standards create expectations around documented ICT risk management, change controls, incident management, resilience and records. OpsProof is designed to help customers operate and evidence selected controls, including change recording/testing/approval/verification, incident timelines, root-cause documentation and operational-resilience evidence.

Approved claim: “OpsProof helps customers implement and evidence selected operational controls relevant to DORA.”

Prohibited claim: “OpsProof makes a customer DORA compliant.”

Customer legal and compliance teams determine applicability, mappings and sufficiency.

#### EU AI Act position

The product must maintain an inventory of AI uses, intended purpose, providers, data, human oversight, logging and risk controls. The company must obtain legal analysis of whether a particular deployment or feature falls into a regulated category. Regardless of classification, the team will apply human oversight, transparency, monitoring, incident and data-governance practices appropriate to enterprise operational decisions.

#### Security and AI frameworks

The engineering system should align with:

- NIST Secure Software Development Framework.
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework and Generative AI Profile.
- OWASP guidance for LLM and generative-AI application risks.
- OpenTelemetry semantic and collection standards where applicable.
- SLSA software supply-chain assurance practices.
- WCAG 2.2 Level AA for the web experience.
- ISO 27001/SOC 2 control expectations as commercial maturity requires.
Alignment means documented implementation and evidence; it does not mean certification unless an accredited assessment has occurred.

### 13.10 Operational resilience

- Critical business services and dependencies are inventoried.
- Platform services have recovery objectives and tested restoration.
- Dependencies, concentration risk and failure modes are reviewed.
- Incident severity, communication and escalation are documented.
- Customer impact and data integrity are prioritized during recovery.

- Recovery exercises include region, identity, event-stream, database, model-provider and connector scenarios.

## 15. Data, analytics, customer outcomes and product telemetry

### 15.1 North-star outcome

Percentage of consequential production changes and incidents that produce a complete, evidence-backed decision record trusted and used by the customer.

This combines coverage, operational usefulness and evidence integrity. It must be decomposed so teams cannot improve it by lowering the definition of “consequential” or “complete.”

### 15.2 Customer outcome metrics

#### Change outcomes

- Change failure rate for in-scope services.
- Rollback/forward-fix rate.
- Percentage of changes assessed before deployment.
- Percentage mapped to correct service and environment.
- Required-control completion rate.
- Waiver rate and repeat-waiver rate.
- High-risk changes deployed with expected controls.
- Verification success, failure and inconclusive rates.
- Time from change creation to defensible decision, separated from customer wait time.

#### Incident outcomes

- Time from incident declaration to first evidence-backed hypothesis.
- Time to confirmed cause or accepted working cause.
- Time to remediation proposal.
- Time to service restoration.
- Number of manual source-system transitions or evidence-gathering actions, measured carefully.
- Confirmed cause rank among initial hypotheses.
- Repeat incident rate by cause/service.
- Percentage of incidents with complete timeline and root-cause record.

#### Evidence and governance outcomes

- Evidence completeness by control and service.
- Manual hours spent assembling audit evidence.
- Percentage of sampled controls with directly retrievable evidence.
- Evidence export validation success.
- Stale service ownership/criticality rate.
- Emergency-change and retrospective-review completion.
- Control effectiveness: association between control presence and outcome, with statistical caution.

### 15.3 Product quality metrics

- Connector event freshness and coverage.
- Entity mapping precision/acceptance and correction rate.
- Risk-assessment reproducibility.
- Policy evaluation error rate.
- Hypothesis evidence-citation precision.
- Generated remediation validation/test pass rate.
- AI unsupported-claim and safety-block rate.
- Evidence integrity validation failures.
- API and UI availability/latency/error.
- Search authorization defects: target zero.
- Accessibility defects by severity.
- Customer-reported trust/usability score for critical workflows.

### 15.4 Adoption metrics

- Active in-scope services and environments.
- Percentage of eligible changes observed.
- Weekly active users by persona, interpreted with workflow frequency.
- Change assessments opened and factor evidence reviewed.
- Policies simulated/activated.
- Incident Rooms used during eligible incidents.
- Diagnostic actions completed.
- Remediation proposals created/accepted/edited/rejected.
- Evidence packets generated and validated.
- Connector/data-quality issues resolved.
Do not treat login frequency as the primary success measure for auditors or executives whose work is episodic.

### 15.5 Commercial and expansion metrics

- Diagnostic-to-pilot conversion.
- Pilot-to-annual-contract conversion.
- Time and effort to deploy a pilot.
- Integration cost per customer.
- Annual recurring revenue and gross retention.
- Net revenue retention.
- Expansion by service, environment, business unit and deployment edition.
- Gross margin including model and support cost.
- Security/procurement cycle blockers.
- Referenceability and quantified customer outcome.

### 15.6 Event taxonomy

#### Naming convention

object.action with consistent past-tense business outcomes where appropriate.

#### Required product events

- connector.created, connector.permission_changed, connector.stale, connector.recovered
- service.discovered, service.mapping_approved, service.mapping_corrected
- change.observed, change.assessment_created, change.assessment_viewed
- control.required, control.satisfied, control.waived, control.failed
- deployment.started, deployment.verified, deployment.failed, deployment.rolled_back
- incident.opened, incident.hypothesis_created, incident.hypothesis_reviewed
- diagnostic.assigned, diagnostic.completed
- remediation.plan_created, remediation.pull_request_created, remediation.deployed
- verification.completed
- evidence.packet_created, evidence.packet_released, evidence.packet_validated
- ai.output_generated, ai.output_accepted, ai.output_edited, ai.output_rejected, ai.output_blocked

#### Event properties

- Tenant and authorized scope identifiers.
- Object identifier/version.
- Role/persona class, avoiding unnecessary personal details.
- Environment and service criticality.
- Source and feature version.
- Outcome/status.
- Latency and error category.
- No raw customer payload in product analytics.

### 15.7 Baseline and attribution

- Establish pre-pilot baselines using customer-approved historical data and interviews.
- Define metric formulas and source before measuring improvement.
- Separate OpsProof processing time from customer approval or execution time.
- Use matched service/time comparisons where possible.
- Avoid claiming causality from simple before/after changes without context.
- Document excluded incidents/changes and data gaps.
- Customer outcome reports distinguish measured facts, estimates and qualitative feedback.

### 15.8 Data governance for analytics

- Analytics events have owner, purpose, retention and classification.
- Customer operational content is excluded unless explicitly necessary and contracted.
- Internal access is role-limited.
- Metric definitions are versioned.
- Dashboards identify data freshness and known gaps.
- Deletion and tenant offboarding propagate to analytics derivatives.
- Cross-customer benchmarks require aggregation thresholds, legal review and contractual permission.

### 16.2 Branching and delivery

- Trunk-based development with short-lived branches.
- Pull requests require tests, owner review and linked requirement/decision.
- Feature flags separate deploy from release.
- Database/event changes are backward-compatible through staged migration.
- Artifacts are built once and promoted.
- Production rollouts are progressive and observable.
- Rollback and forward-fix procedures are defined before release.
- The team dogfoods OpsProof for its own production changes and incidents.

### 16.3 Architecture Decision Records

An ADR is required for:

- New persistent data store.
- New external service/provider.
- New cross-service protocol.
- New production permission or customer-side component.
- Breaking API/schema change.
- New AI model/provider or tool capability.
- Material tenant-isolation change.
- New deployment edition or data-residency model.
- Significant deviation from this specification.
ADRs contain context, decision, alternatives, consequences, security/privacy impact, migration/rollback and owner.

### 16.4 Definition of Ready

A delivery item is ready when:

- User/persona and problem are explicit.
- Desired outcome and non-goals are written.
- Acceptance criteria are testable.
- Data sources and domain objects are identified.
- Authorization and tenant scope are defined.
- Security/privacy classification is complete.
- AI use and fallback are defined where applicable.
- UX states include error, empty, stale and permission cases.
- Telemetry and evidence/audit events are specified.
- Dependencies and operational ownership are understood.
- Design and architecture uncertainty is low enough to start safely.

### 16.5 Definition of Done

An item is done only when:

- Acceptance criteria pass in an integrated environment.
- Unit, contract, integration and required end-to-end tests pass.
- Authorization and tenant-isolation tests pass.
- Threat-model actions are closed or explicitly accepted.
- Accessibility and responsive design are verified.
- Observability dashboards/alerts exist.
- Runbook and support diagnostics exist.
- API/schema documentation is current.
- Data migration, retention and deletion behavior are verified.
- AI evaluations and non-AI fallback pass where applicable.
- Evidence/audit events are correct and immutable where required.
- Performance is within defined budget.
- Feature flag, rollback and kill switch are proven.
- Product, engineering, design, security and operational owner sign off according to risk.

### 16.6 Coding standards

- Strong typing at service and API boundaries.
- No unvalidated dynamic maps for material domain state.
- Errors are typed and preserve cause internally without leaking secrets.
- Context cancellation and timeouts are mandatory for I/O.
- Retries apply only to safe/idempotent operations with budgets and jitter.
- Structured logging with correlation identifiers and classification-safe fields.
- Dependency injection or clear interfaces for external systems.
- Domain logic separated from transport/storage.
- Security-sensitive code has explicit owners and additional review.
- AI prompts and schemas are versioned code artifacts with tests.
- Generated code follows the same review and test standards as human code.

### 16.7 API and event review standards

- Contract-first design for public interfaces.
- Examples and negative cases included.
- Authorization behavior documented per operation.
- Idempotency/concurrency semantics defined.
- Payload size and pagination limits defined.
- Sensitive fields classified.
- Backward compatibility tested.
- Consumer-driven contract tests for internal dependencies.
- Event schemas identify event time, source and lineage.

### 16.8 Database and migration standards

- Migrations are version-controlled and reviewed.
- Expand/migrate/contract pattern for breaking changes.
- Large backfills are resumable, observable and rate-limited.
- Tenant isolation and indexes are verified with query plans.
- Deletion/retention behavior is tested.
- No database trigger contains hidden business logic without documentation.
- Evidence records are append-only through restricted interfaces.
- Restore tests validate schema and integrity.

### 16.9 Feature flags and configuration

- Flags have owner, purpose, scope and expiration/review condition.
- Security boundaries cannot depend only on a client-side flag.
- Tenant flags are audited.
- Default is safest supported behavior.
- Kill switches exist for connectors, AI features, remediation generation and outbound writes.
- Configuration is typed, versioned and validated.
- Remote collector configuration is signed and allowlisted.

### 16.10 Documentation standards

Every customer-facing capability includes:

- Overview and intended outcome.
- Required permissions/data.
- Setup and validation.
- User workflows.
- Limitations and failure behavior.
- Security/privacy notes.
- Troubleshooting.
- API/event reference where applicable.
- Version compatibility and deprecation.
Internal documentation includes architecture, runbooks, threat models, on-call procedures and decision records.

### 16.11 Engineering environments

- Local development uses synthetic data and isolated fixtures.
- Shared development contains no production customer data.
- Staging mirrors production security boundaries and deployment topology where practical.
- Customer pilot data is separated and access-controlled.
- Production access is just-in-time, logged and limited.
- Test tenants include adversarial and high-volume scenarios.
- Seeded incident replays provide deterministic validation.

### 16.12 Operational ownership

Every service has:

- Named engineering owner.
- Service catalog entry.
- SLOs and error budgets.
- Runbook and escalation.
- Dependency inventory.
- Data classification.
- Backup/recovery responsibility.
- Security owner/contact.
- Capacity and cost indicators.
A feature team remains responsible for operability after release; it does not hand defects to a separate operations silo.

### 17.1 Test pyramid and test types

#### Contract tests

- Public OpenAPI requests/responses.

- Connector source fixtures.
- Event schema compatibility.

#### Integration tests

- Database, queue, object store, search and KMS interactions.
- Identity federation and SCIM.
- Git branch/PR flow.
- Kubernetes watch and backfill.
- Terraform plan parsing.
- Observability query constraints.
- Evidence packet build and validation.

- Assess risk and policy.
- Deploy and verify.

- Create remediation pull request.
- Observe customer pipeline and verify recovery.
- Export and validate evidence packet.

#### Non-functional tests

- Performance and capacity.
- Resilience and chaos.
- Security and penetration.
- Accessibility.
- Browser/responsive compatibility.
- Backup/restore and disaster recovery.
- Upgrade/rollback.
- AI robustness and adversarial behavior.

### 17.2 Incident replay harness

The replay harness is a core product asset, not merely a test utility.

#### Requirements

- Load canonical event sets with controlled event and processing times.
- Simulate source lag, missing data, duplicates, out-of-order events and clock skew.
- Replay single and concurrent changes.
- Inject known symptoms and contradictory evidence.
- Define expected topology, risk factors, hypotheses and outcomes.
- Compare engine/model versions.
- Produce human-readable diff report.
- Run locally and in CI with deterministic seeds.
- Support de-identified customer replays under controlled access.

#### Canonical initial scenarios

- Kubernetes memory limit reduction causes OOMKilled and error increase.
- Wrong image/configuration causes failed readiness checks.
- Terraform route/security change causes regional connectivity failure.
- Temporally close deployment is unrelated; third-party outage is actual cause.
- Multiple simultaneous changes affect shared service.
- Observability source is stale and confidence must fall.
- Rollback restores technical health but business KPI remains degraded.
- Generated remediation compiles but violates customer policy.

### 17.3 Risk-engine validation

- Exact expected score for each factor fixture.
- Boundary tests at risk-band transitions.
- Missing data never reduces risk through an unintended default.
- Same snapshot/version yields identical result.
- Model version comparison explains every changed factor.
- Historical similarity excludes leakage from future events.
- Customer customization remains within approved bounds.
- Score distribution and concentration are monitored.
- Qualified domain reviewers assess face validity on replay cases.

### 17.4 Hypothesis-engine validation

- Candidate retrieval recall for known cause.
- Hard-negative rejection.
- Correct topology path.
- Mechanism-symptom compatibility.
- Contradictory evidence penalty.
- Rank stability under duplicate events.
- Appropriate degradation with missing data.
- Human confirmation/rejection history preserved.
- No generated narrative changes the underlying score.

### 17.5 AI evaluation gate

Each AI feature has a written evaluation card containing:

- Intended purpose and prohibited use.
- Input data classes.
- Model/provider/version.
- Prompt and schema version.
- Evaluation datasets.
- Metrics and thresholds.
- Known limitations.
- Human oversight.
- Failure/fallback behavior.
- Monitoring and rollback.
Release requires:

- Zero critical authorization or cross-tenant failures.
- Zero accepted secret leakage in defined test corpus.
- Unsupported factual-claim rate below feature threshold.
- Required structured-output validity.
- Prompt-injection suite pass.
- Human usefulness threshold.
- Cost/latency budget.
- Security and product-safety approval.

### 17.6 Security testing

- Static application and dependency analysis.
- Dynamic API scanning.
- Infrastructure and container scanning.
- Secret scanning.
- Authorization matrix tests.
- Cross-tenant negative tests at API, search, cache, queue and AI retrieval layers.
- Webhook replay/signature tests.
- Connector permission and escape tests.
- SSRF, injection and deserialization tests.
- Evidence tamper and manifest-validation tests.
- Supply-chain provenance verification.
- Independent penetration test before broad enterprise release.

### 17.7 Accessibility and usability testing

- Automated accessibility checks in component and end-to-end CI.
- Keyboard-only review of every core journey.
- Screen-reader testing of Change Detail, Incident Room, Remediation and Evidence Packet.
- Contrast and reflow testing.
- Reduced-motion testing.
- Usability sessions with SRE, change manager and audit personas.
- Stress scenario for Incident Room with noisy timeline.
- Content-comprehension test for risk versus data confidence.

### 17.8 Performance testing

- Webhook/event burst by tenant.
- Sustained connector ingestion.
- Large change with many resources.
- Bounded topology traversal.
- Incident with hundreds/thousands of events.
- Search and evidence packet across long retention.
- AI concurrent requests and provider throttling.
- Database failover and queue backlog recovery.
- Noisy-neighbor tenant isolation.
Performance tests have explicit data sizes and pass criteria; “fast enough” is not acceptable.

### 17.9 Resilience testing

- Connector offline and reconnect.
- Event stream partition/unavailability.
- Database failover.
- Object-store temporary failure.
- Search/index loss and rebuild.
- Identity-provider outage.
- AI provider outage or model refusal spike.
- Regional degradation.
- Partial deployment and rollback failure.
- Clock skew/time service issue.
The UI must reflect degraded capability and data confidence accurately.

### 17.10 Connector certification gate

A connector can be called production-supported only when:

- Supported source versions are defined.
- Least-privilege permissions pass review.
- Authenticity, replay and idempotency tests pass.
- Backfill/incremental sync passes.
- Data classification and redaction pass.
- Source outage/rate limit/credential expiry pass.
- Upgrade/uninstall/deletion pass.
- Synthetic integration runs continuously.
- Monitoring and runbook exist.
- Documentation is customer-tested.

### 17.11 Release gates

#### Product gate

- Outcome and target persona validated.
- Scope/non-goals upheld.
- Acceptance criteria complete.
- Measured customer or replay value.

#### Architecture gate

- Contracts, scalability and failure modes reviewed.
- ADRs complete.
- Migration and rollback proven.

#### Security/privacy gate

- Threat model and data review complete.
- Authorization and isolation tests pass.
- Permissions minimized.
- High-risk findings closed or formally accepted.

#### AI gate

- Evaluation card passes.
- Evidence fidelity and prompt-injection tests pass.
- Fallback and kill switch proven.

#### Evidence gate

- Provenance, hash and history correct.
- Packet/export validation passes.
- Corrections do not mutate history.

#### Reliability gate

- SLO instrumentation, alert and runbook exist.
- Failure and recovery tests pass.

#### Design/accessibility gate

- All states implemented.

#### Customer-operability gate

- Setup, troubleshooting and support path documented.
- Admin can diagnose permissions and freshness.

### 17.12 Paid-pilot readiness gate

The product is ready for a controlled paid pilot when:

- Tenant isolation and core security review pass.
- Required connectors pass certification for the customer's source versions.
- A defined application group can be mapped with acceptable data coverage.

The product council resolves cross-workstream decisions and protects the core loop. Participants include product, engineering, design, security, data/AI, customer implementation and the relevant workstream lead.

- Approve, reject, investigate or defer.

### 18.6 Architecture and security review forum

Triggered by material ADR, connector permission, new data category, write path, model/tool capability or deployment edition.

### 18.7 Customer advisory structure

Use a small group of qualified practitioners and buyers:

- SRE/platform leader.
- Change/ITSM leader.
- Incident commander.
- Technology risk/operational resilience leader.
- Internal audit representative.
- Security architect.
Customer feedback is separated into:

- Problem validation.
- Workflow/usability evidence.
- Product request.
- Procurement/security requirement.
- Bespoke preference.
Only the first four categories should materially influence core scope, and not all requirements deserve product implementation.

### 18.8 Ways of working without a calendar timeline

- Work is pulled according to capability-gate exit criteria and risk.
- Each work item has a directly responsible individual.
- Discovery and delivery remain connected; teams review customer evidence before commitment.
- Design, security and test work begin before implementation, not after code completion.
- Integrated demonstrations use realistic replay data and show failure states.
- Gate reviews occur when evidence is ready, not because a date arrived.
- Incidents trigger blameless learning and tracked corrective actions.
- Architecture and product decisions are recorded at the moment of commitment.
- The backlog is continuously re-ordered by strategic value, trust risk and learning value.

### 18.9 Hiring standards

Prioritize people who:

- Have operated production systems, not only built demos.
- Can explain tradeoffs to engineering and governance users.
- Treat security and evidence integrity as product requirements.
- Are comfortable with incomplete data and honest uncertainty.
- Write tests, runbooks and documentation as part of delivery.
- Avoid uncontrolled platform complexity.
- Can challenge AI output and measure it rigorously.
- Demonstrate empathy during incidents and avoid blame-oriented product design.

## 19. Paid-pilot operating model and commercial packaging

### 19.1 Commercial entry offer - Production Change Risk Diagnostic

#### Purpose

Create immediate paid value, quantify the customer's current problem and determine whether OpsProof can succeed before a broader deployment.

#### Inputs

#### Guardrails

- This is a paid diagnostic, not unpaid consulting to design a custom product.
- Recommendations distinguish configuration, process improvement and product capability.
- Findings use measured evidence and clearly label estimates.

### 19.2 Controlled paid pilot scope

A strong pilot is deliberately narrow:

- One business application group.
- A bounded set of critical technical services.
- One production and one non-production environment where useful.
- GitHub, Kubernetes, Terraform, one observability source and PagerDuty.
- Read-only collection.
- Advisory risk/policy before enforcement.
- Historical incident replay plus live eligible incidents.
- Pull-request remediation only for supported patterns.
- Evidence packet for an agreed control/audit scenario.
The pilot excludes enterprise-wide rollout, unlimited integrations and autonomous production execution.

### 19.3 Pilot entry criteria

- Executive sponsor and technical champion.
- Named service owners and security contact.
- Clear operational pain and at least one historical case.
- Approved data/connector scope.
- Source systems meet supported versions.
- Customer can install/authorize required integrations.
- Success measures have available baseline or agreed proxy.
- Customer agrees to participate in workflow review and outcome validation.
- Commercial path after successful pilot is understood.

### 19.4 Pilot success measures

Select a limited set relevant to the customer:

- Percentage of eligible changes automatically observed and mapped.
- Risk assessments judged useful and explainable by qualified reviewers.
- Time to first evidence-backed incident hypothesis versus baseline.
- Confirmed cause appears within top-ranked hypotheses on historical replays.
- Manual evidence-gathering steps reduced.
- Evidence packet completeness for agreed sample.
- Security and platform teams accept data/permission model.
- At least one operational workflow is adopted rather than only demonstrated.
- Customer identifies a funded expansion path.
Targets must be agreed before activation and include data limitations.

Price should reflect customer value and deployment complexity, not alert volume.

### 20.6 Representative user stories and acceptance criteria

#### Story: assess a Kubernetes change

As a service owner, I need a pre-deployment assessment of a memory-limit reduction so that I can require an appropriate rollout and rollback plan.

Acceptance criteria

- Change is linked to commit, pull request, deployment target and service.
- The exact before/after memory value is shown.
- Risk factor identifies resource reduction and criticality.
- Data confidence identifies missing load test if absent.
- Policy requires canary and owner approval when configured.
- Assessment is immutable and versioned after a new commit.

#### Story: investigate an incident

As an incident commander, I need recent changes ranked by evidence so that the team tests the most plausible causes first.

Acceptance criteria

- Candidate changes are limited to relevant time/environment/topology.
- Each hypothesis shows support, contradiction and missing data.
- A temporally close unrelated change is not ranked above a mechanism-matching cause in the canonical fixture.
- New evidence updates ranking while preserving previous version.
- Only a human can confirm cause.

#### Story: prepare remediation

As a platform engineer, I need a reviewable revert pull request so that recovery uses our existing Git and CI controls.

Acceptance criteria

- User confirms repository, environment and resource.
- Proposed diff is minimal and within allowlist.
- Validation and tests run.
- Risk/policy reassessment is visible.
- Pull request includes evidence and verification plan.
- OpsProof cannot merge or deploy.

#### Story: answer an audit sample

As an internal auditor, I need a complete packet for selected changes so that I can test control operation without requesting screenshots.

Acceptance criteria

- Auditor sees only authorized scope.
- Packet includes request, assessment, approvals, deployment, verification and incident/remediation where applicable.
- Missing evidence is explicit.
- Export includes manifest/checksums and validates independently.
- Redaction creates a new version and preserves source lineage.

### 20.7 Backlog refinement rules

- Every epic maps to a product outcome and gate.
- Technical enablers identify the user/product risk they unlock.
- Stories do not enter delivery without security, data and UX implications.
- Dependencies are explicit; broad “platform” epics are decomposed into testable vertical slices.
- Customer-specific configuration is preferred over forks.
- A work item that cannot state how it will be validated is not ready.

## 21. Risk register, failure modes and mitigation

### 21.1 Strategic and market risks

| Risk | Early signal | Mitigation/decision |
|---|---|---|
| Product is perceived as another observability dashboard. | Buyers compare only to APM features or ask for log storage. | Lead with controlled change, incident evidence and audit record; reject observability replacement scope. |
| Enterprise sales cycle is too slow for runway. | Security/procurement begins before a quantified champion outcome. | Paid diagnostic, narrow pilot, reusable assurance package, target mid-market regulated firms and existing network. |
| Customer pain is process inconvenience, not funded priority. | Users praise demo but no budget owner or paid pilot. | Require paid diagnostic/pilot and executive sponsor; pivot wedge if repeated. |
| Platform is too broad for founding team. | Many connectors/screens exist but closed loop is unreliable. | Enforce P0 loop and capability gates; stop adjacent modules. |
| Incumbents bundle similar features. | Customers accept weaker bundled tool to avoid new vendor. | Differentiate cross-vendor evidence, customer-controlled remediation, policy/evidence depth and deployment flexibility. |
| Founder/team fragmentation. | Resources split across unrelated businesses or custom projects. | One flagship company, explicit capital gates, accountable product council. |

### 21.2 Product and trust risks

| Risk | Early signal | Mitigation/decision |
|---|---|---|
| Risk score lacks credibility. | Users ignore it or challenge unexplained factors. | Deterministic model, factor evidence, customer simulation, separate confidence, domain review. |
| False confidence from incomplete data. | Green/low scores coincide with stale connectors. | Data-confidence pair, assessment-incomplete state, freshness gates. |
| AI hallucination damages incident response. | Unsupported statements are repeated in war room. | Evidence-first UI, strict citation, deterministic engine, evaluation and kill switch. |
| Automation bias causes unsafe approval. | Users accept remediation without evidence/tests. | Human-factors design, required checks, explicit scope confirmation and no merge authority. |
| Evidence integrity is questioned. | Auditor cannot validate or admin can edit history. | Hash/checkpoint, append-only model, signed packet and independent validation. |
| Product adds operational burden. | Engineers duplicate data or bypass workflow. | Automatic ingestion, workflow integration, minimal required manual metadata, measure task effort. |
| Scores become employee blame tools. | Managers request rankings by person/team. | Prohibit performance scoring, focus on system/control outcomes, governance terms and UI safeguards. |

### 21.3 Technical and operational risks

| Risk | Early signal | Mitigation/decision |
|---|---|---|
| Integration maintenance overwhelms core team. | Connector incidents dominate engineering capacity. | Common framework, certification, narrow supported versions, customer evidence before new connector. |
| Service mapping is inaccurate. | High correction rate and misleading blast radius. | Provenance/confidence, explicit correction, bounded graph, pilot readiness threshold. |
| Event volume/cost explodes. | Raw telemetry ingested without decision value. | Metadata-only boundary, query-on-demand, quotas, retention and cost telemetry. |
| Multi-tenant isolation defect. | Authorization inconsistency or shared-cache leak. | Defense in depth, automated negative tests, high-isolation option, security review. |
| Customer environment blocks collector. | Long security review or network incompatibility. | Outbound-only, transparent permissions, agentless APIs where possible, private connectivity. |
| Private deployments create product forks. | Customer-specific patches and upgrade failures. | Portable standard edition, configuration not fork, strict support matrix and commercial pricing. |
| AI provider outage/cost volatility. | Feature latency/failure or margin erosion. | Gateway, fallback, quotas, deterministic core, private model options and cost budgets. |
| Evidence storage growth harms margin. | Long retention and packet data increase cost. | Bounded payloads, tiered storage, lifecycle, pricing and customer-configurable retention. |

### 21.4 Security and legal risks

| Risk | Early signal | Mitigation/decision |
|---|---|---|
| Excessive customer permissions. | Security reviewers reject collector or Git write. | Read-only default, optional scoped PR write, permission diff and independent review. |
| Sensitive data enters prompts/logs. | Secret scanner alerts or customer concern. | Local redaction, exclusion defaults, DLP checks, no raw logs, model routing policy. |
| Compliance overclaim creates liability. | Sales says “DORA compliant.” | Approved claims, legal review and training; product evidence not certification. |
| Customer data used beyond purpose. | Ambiguous provider retention or analytics reuse. | Contractual controls, inventory, opt-in, isolation and deletion propagation. |
| Supply-chain compromise. | Unverified image/dependency or build anomaly. | SBOM, signing, provenance, isolated builds, dependency policy and monitoring. |

### 21.5 Organizational risks

- Product, engineering and design operate as sequential handoffs.
- Security is consulted only before procurement.
- Sales commits unsupported features.
- Customer implementation hides product defects with manual work.
- AI team optimizes model benchmarks without user outcome.
- Platform team builds abstractions before a real product slice.
- Teams avoid killing low-value work because of sunk cost.
Mitigation is embedded in shared gates, decision rights, integrated demos, paid validation and explicit non-goals.

### 21.6 Kill, pause and pivot signals

The company should reconsider the wedge or investment level when repeated qualified customer evidence shows:

- No willingness to pay for diagnostic or pilot.
- Buyers cannot identify a budget owner despite material incidents.
- Required permissions/data are consistently unacceptable to target customers.
- Service mapping cannot reach useful accuracy with economically sustainable effort.
- Incident hypotheses do not materially improve responder workflow on validated cases.
- Evidence value is appreciated only by auditors but does not secure operational sponsorship.
- Integration/implementation cost prevents viable gross margin.
- Incumbent bundled products satisfy the same outcome with low switching cost.
The response is not to add more features automatically. Diagnose whether the problem, customer segment, deployment model or product boundary is wrong.

### 21.7 Capital discipline gates

- Spend on core product only after qualified problem interviews support the wedge.
- Increase product investment after paid diagnostic/design partners.
- Expand team after repeatable pilot outcomes and clear ownership gaps.
- Add connectors after multiple customer demand or strategic necessity.
- Build private deployment after committed economic demand.
- Introduce production execution authority only after trust, security, customer policy and liability case are proven.

- ☐ Exact action types and resource scopes are allowlisted.
- ☐ Customer business value cannot be achieved adequately through Git/CI/CD pull request.
- ☐ Separate customer opt-in exists by tenant, environment, service and action.
- ☐ Credential is least-privilege, short-lived and customer-revocable.

- ☐ Deterministic policy gate precedes action.
- ☐ AI cannot expand scope or select unapproved tools.
- ☐ Dry run/plan is available and human-readable.
- ☐ Automated tests and blast-radius analysis pass.
- ☐ Rollback is defined and tested.
- ☐ Rate, concurrency and cumulative-impact limits exist.
- ☐ Kill switch is tested.
- ☐ Every action and source evidence is immutably logged.
- ☐ Customer receives immediate action visibility.
- ☐ Incident and liability response is defined.
- ☐ Independent penetration and abuse testing passes.
- ☐ Executive product, engineering and security approval is recorded.

### Appendix L - Final team directive

The team is building one trustworthy operational decision system, not a collection of AI demos.

For every consequential change and incident, OpsProof must be able to answer:

- What happened and where did the information come from?
- Which business and technical services were exposed?
- What risk and uncertainty existed before execution?
- Which customer policies and controls applied?
- What evidence supports or contradicts the incident hypotheses?
- Who authorized the remediation and through which controlled path?
- Did the system recover according to defined indicators?
- Can the full record be validated afterward?
When forced to choose, choose:

- Evidence over fluency.
- Customer control over impressive autonomy.
- Deterministic behavior over opaque scoring.
- A complete closed loop over a broad feature list.
- Least privilege over integration convenience.
- Honest uncertainty over false confidence.
- Repeatable product architecture over bespoke customer work.
The product is ready to scale only when customers trust it during both the calm before a deployment and the pressure of a live incident.
